Every day our bodies meet millions of germs, such as bacteria and viruses. Most of them never make us ill, because we have a powerful defence system called the immune system. Vaccines are one of the cleverest ideas in medicine. They work by teaching this defence system what a dangerous germ looks like before the germ ever has a chance to attack.

## Our Natural Defence System

When a new germ enters the body, the immune system notices that it does not belong. Special white blood cells attack it, and other cells make proteins called antibodies. Antibodies stick to the germ and help destroy it. This fight is why we sometimes get a fever or feel tired when we are ill.

The most amazing part comes afterwards. Once the battle is over, the body keeps some memory cells. These cells remember the germ. If the same germ returns, the memory cells recognise it straight away and the body can fight back much faster, often before we even feel sick. This is why many people catch diseases like chickenpox only once. Scientists call this lasting protection immunity, and it can last for many years or even a whole lifetime.

## How a Vaccine Trains the Body

The problem is that the first fight against a dangerous germ can be very serious. A vaccine gives the immune system a safe practice session. It contains a weakened or killed germ, a small piece of the germ, or instructions that help our cells make one harmless piece of it.

The immune system reacts to this harmless sample as if it were the real thing. It makes antibodies and memory cells, but the person does not get the full disease. Later, if the real germ arrives, the body is already prepared. Some vaccines need more than one dose, or a booster after a few years, to keep the memory strong.

## A Short History of Vaccines

The story of vaccines began with smallpox, a terrible disease that killed millions of people. In 1796 an English doctor called Edward Jenner noticed that milkmaids who caught a mild illness called cowpox did not get smallpox. He tested his idea and found that cowpox could protect people. The word vaccine comes from the Latin word for cow.

Over the following two centuries, scientists created vaccines against polio, measles, tetanus and many other diseases. A worldwide vaccination campaign was so successful that smallpox was declared completely eradicated in 1980. It is the first human disease ever to be wiped out. In recent years, new vaccine technologies were developed very quickly during the COVID-19 pandemic.

## Protecting Each Other

Vaccines do not only protect the person who receives them. When most people in a community are vaccinated, a germ finds it hard to spread from person to person. This is called herd immunity. It protects newborn babies, very old people and those with weak immune systems who cannot always be vaccinated themselves.

Like all medicines, vaccines can cause side effects, such as a sore arm or a mild fever for a day or two. Serious side effects are very rare. Before a vaccine is approved, it is tested on thousands of volunteers, and it continues to be monitored after it is in use. Doctors agree that for approved vaccines the benefits are far greater than the risks.

## Conclusion

Vaccines work by giving the immune system a safe preview of a dangerous germ, so that it can remember and respond quickly in the future. From Jenner's early experiment to the end of smallpox, vaccines have saved millions of lives. They are a powerful example of how understanding our own bodies can help science protect whole communities.
