On Christmas Day in 2021, a rocket lifted off from French Guiana in South America carrying one of the most complicated machines ever built. It was the James Webb Space Telescope, often simply called Webb. Since then, it has sent back pictures so detailed and beautiful that they have changed how scientists and ordinary people see the universe.

## A Giant Golden Eye

Webb was built by NASA together with the European Space Agency and the Canadian Space Agency. Its main mirror is 6.5 metres wide, much bigger than the mirror of the famous Hubble Space Telescope. The mirror is made of 18 six-sided pieces covered in a very thin layer of gold, which reflects infrared light extremely well.

The mirror was too big to fit inside a rocket, so engineers designed the telescope to fold up like origami. After launch, it slowly unfolded in space over several weeks. Hundreds of parts had to work perfectly, and there was no way to send astronauts to repair it. Many engineers said those weeks were the most stressful of their lives. Everything worked.

## Why Infrared?

Our eyes see visible light, the colours of the rainbow. Webb is designed mainly to see infrared light, which is a kind of light our eyes cannot detect but which we feel as heat. Infrared light has two big advantages for astronomers.

First, it can pass through clouds of dust in space that block visible light. This lets Webb see stars and planets being born inside dusty clouds. Second, light from the most distant galaxies has been stretched into infrared as the universe expanded. To see the very first galaxies, a telescope must see infrared.

Because infrared is heat, the telescope itself must be extremely cold, or its own warmth would ruin the pictures. Webb has a sunshield about the size of a tennis court, made of five thin layers. One side faces the Sun and is hot, while the side with the mirror stays at around minus 230 degrees Celsius. Webb orbits a special point in space about 1.5 million kilometres from Earth, where it can keep the Sun, Earth and Moon behind its shield.

## Looking Back in Time

Light takes time to travel. When we look at the Sun, we see it as it was about eight minutes ago. When Webb looks at a galaxy billions of light years away, it sees light that left that galaxy billions of years ago. In other words, looking far away means looking back in time.

Webb has found galaxies that existed only a few hundred million years after the Big Bang, which is very young in cosmic terms. Some of them seem brighter and more developed than scientists expected, and researchers are now working to understand why. Its first full-colour image, released in July 2022, showed thousands of galaxies in a tiny patch of sky.

## Searching for Other Worlds

Webb also studies planets around other stars, called exoplanets. When a planet passes in front of its star, some starlight shines through the planet's atmosphere. Webb can split that light into colours and work out which gases are there, such as water vapour or carbon dioxide. This is an important step in the long search for worlds where life might be possible. Webb cannot prove that life exists elsewhere, but it can tell us which planets are worth studying more closely.

## Conclusion

The James Webb Space Telescope is a triumph of international teamwork and careful engineering. By seeing in infrared, it can peer through dust, watch stars being born and look back towards the beginning of time. Its discoveries are raising new questions, and it will keep exploring and surprising us for many years to come.
