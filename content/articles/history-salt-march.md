Salt is something we use every day without thinking about it. But in 1930, a handful of salt became a powerful symbol of freedom. Mahatma Gandhi, the leader of India's independence movement, used it to challenge one of the largest empires in the world. The event, known as the Salt March or the Dandi March, became one of the most famous protests in history.

## Why Salt?

At that time, India was ruled by Britain. British laws said that only the government could make or sell salt, and it charged a heavy tax on it. Indians were not allowed to collect salt from the sea, even though it lay freely along the coast. Everyone needs salt, especially in a hot country where people sweat a lot while working. The tax affected the poorest people most.

Many leaders were surprised when Gandhi chose salt as the focus of a national protest. They expected something larger, like a demand about laws or elections. But Gandhi understood that salt was simple and touched every single Indian, rich or poor, of every religion and region. It was an issue that everyone could understand and support.

## The Long Walk to Dandi

On 12 March 1930, Gandhi set out from Sabarmati Ashram near Ahmedabad with 78 followers. Their goal was the small coastal village of Dandi in Gujarat, about 385 kilometres away. Gandhi was sixty years old, yet he walked around fifteen to twenty kilometres every day.

Along the way, he stopped in villages and spoke to large crowds. He explained why the salt law was unjust and asked people to join the movement peacefully. Newspapers in India and around the world reported on the march every day. The group grew as thousands of people joined them on the road.

On 6 April 1930, after twenty-four days, Gandhi reached the beach at Dandi. He bent down and picked up a lump of natural salt, breaking the law. It was a simple action, but its message was enormous.

## Non-violence in Action

Gandhi's method was called satyagraha, which means holding firmly to truth. It meant resisting injustice without using violence, and accepting punishment calmly. After Dandi, people across India began making and selling salt illegally. Many also refused to buy British cloth.

The government responded with mass arrests. Tens of thousands of people were jailed, including Gandhi himself. At Dharasana, peaceful protesters walked towards a salt works and were beaten by police, yet they did not hit back. Reports of this brutality shocked readers around the world and increased sympathy for India's cause.

## The Legacy of the March

The Salt March did not bring independence immediately. India became free in 1947, seventeen years later. But the march united millions of Indians and showed that ordinary people could challenge powerful rulers without weapons. It forced the British government to negotiate with Indian leaders. In 1931, Gandhi travelled to London for talks about India's future, which showed how much the protest had changed the situation. Women also played a major role in the salt movement. Leaders such as Sarojini Naidu led protests and inspired many other women to step forward into public life.

Its influence spread far beyond India. Leaders such as Martin Luther King Jr. in the United States and Nelson Mandela in South Africa studied Gandhi's ideas about non-violent resistance. Peaceful protest movements around the world still use similar methods today.

## Conclusion

The Salt March turned a small, everyday item into a symbol of freedom. Through careful planning, simple language and great courage, Gandhi showed that peaceful action could move a whole nation. It remains a powerful lesson in leadership, communication and standing up for what is right.
