Long before the pyramids of Egypt were finished, a remarkable civilisation was growing along the rivers of what is now Pakistan and north-west India. It is called the Indus Valley Civilisation, or the Harappan Civilisation, after the first city that archaeologists discovered. Its people built some of the best planned cities of the ancient world, and yet much about them is still a mystery.

## Discovery of a Lost World

For thousands of years, this civilisation was forgotten. In the 1920s, archaeologists digging at Harappa in Punjab and at Mohenjo-daro in Sindh realised they had found something extraordinary. These were not small villages but large cities, with thousands of people. Since then, more than a thousand sites have been found, including Dholavira and Lothal in Gujarat and Rakhigarhi in Haryana.

The civilisation was at its height from about 2600 to 1900 BCE. At that time, it covered a larger area than ancient Egypt or Mesopotamia, the other great early civilisations. Its people lived mainly by farming. They grew wheat, barley and cotton, and they kept cattle, sheep and goats.

## Cities Ahead of Their Time

What amazes visitors most is how carefully these cities were planned. Streets were laid out in a grid pattern, crossing each other at right angles, much like a modern city. Houses were built from baked bricks of standard sizes, which suggests that people across a huge region followed the same measurements.

Many houses had their own wells and bathrooms. Waste water flowed into covered drains along the streets, a system of public hygiene that many cities in the world did not have until thousands of years later. In Mohenjo-daro there is a large public pool known as the Great Bath. It was carefully waterproofed and may have been used for religious ceremonies or special bathing rituals. Lothal had what may have been a dockyard, connected to the sea by a channel.

## Traders and Craftspeople

The Harappans were skilled craftspeople. They made beautiful beads from stones, pottery with painted designs, tools of copper and bronze, and small figures such as the famous bronze dancing girl from Mohenjo-daro. They used standard stone weights for trade, which shows that they valued fairness and organisation.

They also traded over long distances. Seals and beads from the Indus region have been found in Mesopotamia, in modern Iraq, which shows that goods travelled by land and sea. Their small square seals often show animals like a bull or an elephant, along with short lines of symbols.

## An Unsolved Puzzle

Those symbols are part of the Indus script, and here lies one of the great mysteries of history. Nobody has managed to read it. The inscriptions are very short, and there is no bilingual text, like Egypt's Rosetta Stone, to help experts crack the code. Because of this, we do not know the names of their kings, the stories they told or the language they spoke.

Around 1900 BCE, the big cities slowly declined and people moved to smaller settlements. Historians are not sure why. Possible reasons include changes in the climate, rivers drying up or changing course, and a drop in trade. It was probably a combination of causes rather than a sudden disaster. The people did not simply vanish, and many of their farming methods, crafts and traditions may have continued in later cultures of South Asia.

## Conclusion

The Indus Valley Civilisation shows that people more than four thousand years ago could plan cities, manage water and trade across great distances. Its brick streets and drains still impress engineers today. Until someone finally reads its script, it will remain one of the most fascinating puzzles of the ancient world.
