Long before cartoons and storybooks, children in India learned about life through stories about clever rabbits, foolish lions, loyal friends and tricky jackals. Many of these stories come from the Panchatantra, one of the oldest and most widely travelled collections of stories in the world. More than two thousand years after it was created, its tales are still told by grandparents, printed in comics and used in classrooms.

## The Story Behind the Stories

The Panchatantra was probably composed in Sanskrit around two thousand years ago, although nobody knows the exact date. Its name means five books or five principles. According to tradition, it was written by a wise teacher named Vishnu Sharma.

The book begins with its own story. A king was worried because his three sons were lazy and uninterested in learning. Many teachers had failed. Vishnu Sharma promised to make the princes wise in just six months. Instead of dull lectures, he taught them through entertaining stories. The princes loved the tales, and through them they learned how to make good decisions, choose friends wisely and handle difficult situations.

## How the Book Works

The Panchatantra teaches niti, which means practical wisdom for living well in the world. It is not mainly about religion or rules. Instead, it shows how to survive and succeed by using intelligence and good judgement.

The five books have different themes: losing friends, gaining friends, war and peace, losing what you have gained, and acting without thinking. The stories are cleverly arranged one inside another. A character in one story often says, let me tell you a tale, and starts a new story. This style keeps listeners curious and connects many lessons together.

## Famous Tales

In The Monkey and the Crocodile, a monkey living on a riverbank shares sweet fruit with a crocodile and becomes his friend. The crocodile's wife wants to eat the monkey's heart, so the crocodile invites him across the river on his back. Halfway across, he reveals the plan. The quick-thinking monkey says he has left his heart on the tree and must go back for it. Once on the bank, he climbs to safety. The lesson is to stay calm and think quickly in danger.

In The Lion and the Clever Rabbit, a fierce lion eats too many animals in the forest, so they agree to send him one animal each day. When it is a small rabbit's turn, he arrives late and tells the lion that another lion stopped him. He leads the angry lion to a deep well, where the lion sees his own reflection, jumps in to fight it and drowns. Intelligence, the story shows, can defeat strength.

Another well-known tale, The Tortoise and the Geese, tells of a tortoise who cannot stop talking. Two geese carry him through the sky on a stick, but he opens his mouth to speak and falls. Sometimes, it reminds us, it is wiser to stay silent.

## A Journey Around the World

The Panchatantra became one of the most translated books of the ancient world. Around the sixth century, it was translated into Persian, and later into Arabic as Kalila wa Dimna, named after two jackals in the stories. From there it travelled into Greek, Hebrew, Latin and many European languages. Some scholars believe it influenced later fable writers in Europe. Today, versions exist in dozens of languages.

## Conclusion

The Panchatantra shows that stories can be one of the best teachers. Through talking animals and simple plots, it teaches lessons about friendship, trust, courage and clear thinking. Its wisdom crossed continents and centuries, and it continues to delight and guide readers of every age.
