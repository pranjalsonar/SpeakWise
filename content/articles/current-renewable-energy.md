Every time we switch on a light, charge a phone or ride a bus, we use energy. For more than two hundred years, most of that energy has come from burning coal, oil and natural gas. These fossil fuels built the modern world, but they have also created one of its biggest problems: climate change. The search for cleaner energy has become one of the most important stories of our time.

## Why Fossil Fuels Are a Problem

When fossil fuels burn, they release carbon dioxide into the air. Carbon dioxide is a greenhouse gas, which means it traps heat in the atmosphere, a little like a blanket around the Earth. As the amount of these gases has grown, the planet has warmed. The world is now more than one degree Celsius warmer than before the Industrial Revolution.

That may not sound like much, but it has big effects. Heatwaves are becoming more frequent and intense. Glaciers are melting and sea levels are rising. Rainfall is becoming less predictable, with more floods in some places and droughts in others. Farmers, coastal cities and poor communities are often hit hardest.

In 2015, almost every country in the world signed the Paris Agreement. They agreed to try to keep global warming well below two degrees, and ideally to limit it to one and a half degrees. To do that, the world must cut emissions sharply, and the energy sector is the biggest place to start.

## The Rise of Renewables

Renewable energy comes from sources that do not run out, such as sunlight, wind, flowing water and heat from inside the Earth. The biggest success story of the last decade has been solar and wind power. Their costs have fallen dramatically. In many parts of the world, building a new solar or wind farm is now cheaper than building a new coal power station.

India is one of the countries investing heavily. It has huge solar parks in states such as Rajasthan and Gujarat, and many homes and offices now have solar panels on their roofs. The government has set a target of 500 gigawatts of electricity capacity from non-fossil sources by 2030. Electric scooters, cars and buses are also becoming more common on Indian roads.

## The Challenges

Switching to renewables is not simple. The sun does not shine at night and the wind does not always blow. To keep the lights on, we need ways to store energy, such as large batteries or pumped water storage, and better electricity grids that can move power from one region to another.

Building solar panels, wind turbines and batteries also needs minerals like lithium and copper. Mining them must be done responsibly to avoid harming people and nature. There is also the question of fairness. Many towns depend on coal mines and power plants for jobs. Experts talk about a just transition, which means helping these workers retrain and find new work so they are not left behind.

Developing countries point out that rich countries have produced most of the emissions so far. They argue that wealthy nations should help pay for clean energy and climate protection in poorer countries, while those countries continue to grow.

## What Individuals Can Do

Big changes depend on governments and companies, but individuals can help too. Saving electricity, using public transport, walking or cycling for short trips and avoiding waste all reduce emissions. Talking about climate issues and supporting good policies also matters.

## Conclusion

The move from fossil fuels to renewable energy is one of the biggest changes the world has ever attempted. Cheaper solar and wind power give real reasons for hope. If countries work together and make the change fair, clean energy can protect the planet while bringing jobs, cleaner air and a better future for everyone.
