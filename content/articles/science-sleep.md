Most of us spend about one third of our lives asleep. That can sound like a lot of wasted time. Why would the body switch off for so many hours every night? Scientists now know that sleep is not a pause at all. It is a busy time when the brain and body do important work that they cannot do while we are awake.

## What Happens When We Sleep

Sleep is not one long, flat state. Every night we move through cycles that last about ninety minutes. Each cycle has lighter sleep, deep sleep and a stage called REM sleep. REM stands for rapid eye movement, because our eyes move quickly under the eyelids. Most vivid dreams happen during REM sleep.

In deep sleep, the heart rate and breathing slow down. The body releases growth hormone, which helps to repair muscles and tissues. This is one reason children and teenagers need so much sleep, because their bodies are still growing. Later in the night, REM periods become longer, and the brain becomes very active again.

## Sleep and the Brain

One of the most important jobs of sleep is helping us learn. During the day we take in a huge amount of information. At night, the brain sorts through it. It keeps useful memories, makes them stronger and lets less important details fade. Students who sleep well after studying usually remember more than students who stay up all night.

Sleep also works like a cleaning service. Research suggests that during deep sleep, fluid flows through the brain and washes away waste products that build up during the day. Some of these waste products are linked to diseases of the brain in later life. So a good night's sleep may protect the brain for many years.

## How Much Sleep Do We Need?

The answer changes with age. Babies sleep for most of the day. Teenagers usually need around eight to ten hours. Most adults need seven to nine hours each night. Some people believe they can train themselves to need less sleep, but for almost everyone this is not true. They simply get used to feeling tired.

When we do not get enough sleep, the effects appear quickly. We find it harder to concentrate, we make more mistakes and we feel more irritable. Over a long time, poor sleep is linked to weight gain, heart problems, a weaker immune system and low mood. Tired drivers are also a serious danger on the roads, because a sleepy brain reacts much more slowly.

## Building Better Sleep Habits

The good news is that small changes can make a big difference. Going to bed and waking up at the same time every day, even at weekends, helps to set the body clock. Bright light in the morning tells the body that the day has started.

In the evening, it helps to keep the bedroom dark, quiet and cool. Phones and laptops give off blue light, which can trick the brain into thinking it is still daytime, so it is better to put screens away before bed. Large meals, tea, coffee and energy drinks late in the day can also keep us awake. A calm routine, like reading a book or listening to soft music, prepares the mind for rest.

## Conclusion

Sleep is one of the most powerful tools we have for a healthy body and a sharp mind. It helps us learn, repairs our bodies and protects our mood. Instead of seeing sleep as lost time, we should treat it as an investment. When we sleep well, we are able to think, work and speak at our best the next day.
