Climate Change: Causes, Effects and Responses

Climate change refers to long-term shifts in the Earth's temperature and weather patterns. Some of these shifts are natural, but since the industrial revolution human activity has become the main driver. The central cause is the burning of fossil fuels such as coal, oil and natural gas for electricity, transport and industry.

When fossil fuels burn, they release carbon dioxide into the atmosphere. Carbon dioxide, methane and nitrous oxide are called greenhouse gases because they trap heat that would otherwise escape into space. This is similar to how the glass of a greenhouse keeps the inside warm. As the concentration of these gases rises, the planet's average temperature rises with it.

Deforestation makes the problem worse. Trees absorb carbon dioxide as they grow, so forests act as natural carbon stores. When forests are cleared for farming or construction, that storage capacity is lost, and burning or decaying wood releases the carbon it held. Agriculture also contributes: livestock such as cattle produce methane, and some fertilisers release nitrous oxide.

One of the most visible effects of warming is melting ice. Glaciers in mountain ranges are shrinking, and the ice sheets of Greenland and Antarctica are losing mass. Meltwater flowing into the oceans, together with the fact that water expands as it warms, causes sea levels to rise. Rising seas threaten coastal cities, low-lying islands and river deltas where millions of people live and farm.

Climate change also makes extreme weather more frequent and more intense in many regions. Heatwaves are becoming hotter and longer. Some areas face more severe droughts, while others experience heavier rainfall and flooding. Warmer oceans can strengthen tropical storms. These events damage homes, crops and infrastructure, and they place heavy costs on economies and public health systems.

Ecosystems are under pressure as well. Many plant and animal species are adapted to particular temperature ranges and must move or adapt as conditions change. Coral reefs are especially sensitive: when the water becomes too warm, corals expel the algae that live inside them and turn white, a process called bleaching. Repeated bleaching can kill large areas of reef.

The effects of climate change are not shared equally. Poorer countries and communities often have contributed least to emissions, yet they are frequently the most exposed to floods, droughts and crop failures and have fewer resources to adapt. This raises questions of fairness and responsibility between nations and between generations.

Reducing emissions is called mitigation. The most important step is to shift energy production from fossil fuels to low-carbon sources. Renewable energy from solar panels, wind turbines and hydroelectric dams generates electricity without burning fuel, and the cost of solar and wind power has fallen considerably in recent years. Improving energy efficiency in buildings, appliances and vehicles also reduces the amount of energy needed in the first place.

Transport is a major source of emissions. Electric vehicles, better public transport, cycling and walking can all reduce the use of petrol and diesel. Protecting existing forests and planting new ones helps to remove carbon dioxide from the air.

Adaptation means preparing for the changes that can no longer be avoided. Examples include building flood defences, designing buildings that stay cool during heatwaves, developing drought-resistant crops and improving early-warning systems for storms.

Governments play a central role. They can put a price on carbon through taxes or trading schemes, invest in clean energy and set standards for vehicles and buildings. International cooperation is also needed because greenhouse gases mix across the whole atmosphere. Under the Paris Agreement of 2015, countries agreed to try to keep global warming well below two degrees Celsius above pre-industrial levels and to pursue efforts to limit it to one and a half degrees.

Individuals can contribute too, for example by saving electricity, choosing public transport, reducing food waste and eating less meat. Individual actions alone cannot solve the problem, but they add up and can influence businesses and governments. Climate change is a long-term challenge, and the choices made in the coming decades will shape the world that future generations inherit.
