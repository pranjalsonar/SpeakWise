Social Media and Young People: Benefits, Risks and Healthy Habits

Social media platforms allow people to create profiles, share posts, photos and videos, and interact with others online. For many young people, platforms such as Instagram, TikTok, YouTube and Snapchat are a central part of daily life. Surveys in many countries find that most teenagers use social media every day, and a large share say they are online almost constantly. Because these platforms have become so common so quickly, researchers, parents and governments are trying to understand how they affect young people's wellbeing.

How social media works

Most social media platforms are free to use. They earn money by selling advertising, so their business depends on keeping users engaged for as long as possible. To do this, platforms use recommendation algorithms that learn from what each person watches, likes and shares, and then show them more content that is likely to hold their attention. Features such as infinite scrolling, autoplay videos, notifications and visible counts of likes and followers are designed to encourage people to keep returning.

This design is not necessarily harmful in itself. The same systems that keep people scrolling also help them discover music, ideas and communities they would never have found otherwise. However, it means that the amount of time people spend on these platforms is shaped by powerful design choices, not only by their own decisions.

The benefits

Social media can help young people stay connected with friends and family, especially those who live far away. It allows people who feel isolated, such as young people with rare illnesses, disabilities or minority identities, to find others with similar experiences and to feel less alone. Many teenagers say social media helps them feel more connected to what is happening in their friends' lives and gives them a place to express their creativity.

Social media is also a source of information and learning. Students use video platforms to learn skills, from mathematics to musical instruments. Young people have used social media to organise around causes they care about, such as climate action, and to raise money for charity. For some, building an audience online has even become the start of a career.

The risks

Researchers have also identified several risks. One concern is the effect on mental health. Some studies find links between heavy social media use and higher rates of anxiety, depression and low self-esteem, especially among teenage girls. Constantly seeing carefully edited images of other people's lives can lead to unfavourable comparisons about appearance, popularity and success. However, the evidence is debated. Many studies show only a small average effect, and it is difficult to tell whether social media causes poor mental health or whether young people who are already struggling use social media more. Effects also seem to vary a lot between individuals and depend on how platforms are used.

A second concern is sleep. Using phones late at night delays bedtime, and notifications can wake people up. Because sleep is essential for memory, mood and concentration, losing sleep can affect school performance and wellbeing. Many experts recommend keeping phones out of the bedroom at night.

A third concern is harmful content and contact. Young people can be exposed to cyberbullying, hateful content, misinformation, or content promoting dangerous behaviours such as extreme dieting or self-harm. Recommendation algorithms can sometimes lead users from ordinary content to more extreme material. There is also the risk of contact with strangers who may try to exploit young people.

Finally, there are concerns about privacy. Platforms collect large amounts of data about their users, including their interests, locations and contacts. Young people may share personal information or images without fully understanding who can see them or how long they will remain online.

What is being done

Governments in several countries have introduced or proposed laws to make platforms safer for young users. These include age-verification requirements, limits on collecting children's data, requirements to remove illegal content quickly, and rules that make platforms assess the risks their design creates for children. Some places have debated banning social media for children under a certain age, while others argue that education and better design are more effective than bans.

Platforms have added features such as screen-time reminders, private accounts by default for younger users, options to hide like counts and tools for parents. Critics argue that these features are often hard to find and that real change requires altering the business model that rewards maximum engagement.

Healthy habits

Experts generally suggest that the question is not simply how much time young people spend online, but how they use it and what it replaces. Active use, such as chatting with friends or creating something, tends to be more positive than passively scrolling. Practical habits include turning off non-essential notifications, setting time limits, keeping phones out of the bedroom at night, curating feeds to follow accounts that are uplifting or educational, and taking regular breaks. It also helps to remember that what people post is usually a selected and edited version of their lives.

Schools increasingly teach digital literacy, helping students recognise misinformation, protect their privacy and respond to online bullying. Open conversations between young people and adults about online experiences, without immediate punishment, make it more likely that young people will ask for help when something goes wrong.

Conclusion

Social media is neither purely good nor purely bad. It offers real opportunities for connection, creativity and learning, and real risks to sleep, mental health and safety. The overall effect depends on the individual, the platform design and the way it is used. Making social media healthier for young people is likely to require effort from platforms, governments, schools, families and young people themselves.
